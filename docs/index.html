AbstractClaude Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 attempted 30 GDPval gold tasks in six occupations twice (132 deliverables, all delivered). Two LLM judges, GPT-6.1 and Gemini 4 Argon, graded them requirement by requirement; a seeded random sample of 22 was reviewed from packets of source and output text. The review identified a consequential error in 7 outputs. The GPT-6.1 judge names 5 in its verdict but scores 1; Gemini names 1; neither rates any at severity 4, and both fail most outputs where the review found no consequential error. Cost: $149.73.

Three findings

7 / 22outputs with an error found in review

Drawn by seed, frozen before this run's grading, then labeled from the request and the source and output text in each review packet (Sonnet 5 of 12, Opus 2 of 10; the packet cut 9 long spreadsheets, see Limits). 6 of the 7 sit in the approval-heavy occupations of the first stage, where 6 of 10 sampled outputs fail; the three new occupations, mostly calculation and data work, yield 1 of 12. The first stage's signature, a rule stated correctly in one place and broken in the operative one, appears in 2 of the 7. Three of the 22 are web-research tasks, which the pre-registration reports separately; one of the seven is among them, so the native-file figure is 6 of 19 (CI 15–54%).

Errors concentrate where the document commits someone to act: a total, a status claim, an approval route, a stock move, a carrier choice.

5 / 7errors the GPT-6.1 judge names; 1 is scored

Read over the whole judgment, the GPT-6.1 judge names 5 of the 7: the ₹12m understatement, the extra PM Lead signature, the twice-halved receipt and the non-standard carrier in its overall verdict, and the Class A approval route in a failed requirement. Only that last one is scored, at severity 3; the four in the verdict carry no requirement and no severity. It misses the notices reported as sent and the projected sample. The Gemini judge names only the Class A route, plus a related stock error on another item. At severity 4, the rubric's own line for "consequential", neither judge flags any of the 7 or any of the 15 outputs where the review found no consequential error. Under the grader's pre-registered rule (critical requirement or severity ≥ 4) the GPT-6.1 judge flags 5 of the 7 documents but also fails 7 of 15 outputs where the review found no consequential error; at any failed requirement, 7 and 12.

The judge often sees the error; the score drops it. What a grader lacks is the error tied to a requirement, located and graded for consequence.

10 / 12consistent new-occupation pairs, Opus preferred (Gemini)

On the 15 new-occupation Sonnet-vs-Opus pairs, judged GDPval-style in both presentation orders, Gemini returns a position-consistent verdict on 12 and picks Opus in 10 of them; the GPT-6.1 judge is consistent on 14 and picks Opus in 10. Where the rubric rates the two outputs differently, the preference judge picks the more severe one in 2 of 5 pairs (Gemini) and 1 of 1 (GPT-6.1). Opus did have fewer confirmed errors here (2 of 10 against 5 of 12).

Preference can rank the models right and still certify a broken document. It measures which is better, not whether either is usable.

Before this run

A first stage ran 15 of these tasks (three occupations) through three models, 60 + 28 attempts, with 8 full-output reads by the author: all 53 parseable outputs passed targeted fact checks, 4 of the 8 read in full carried a consequential error, and 24 of 35 failed attempts were the setup rather than the model. GPT-6.1 also ran as a solver there. Its 25 parseable outputs account for 0 of the 11 model-behavior failures in the triage, and the 5 of them read in full for this run's confound check carry no consequential error (95% CI 0–43%). Its own judge flagged 0 of its 25 outputs against 10 of 28 Claude outputs (Fisher p = 0.0008), so that clean record is not yet separable from self-preference. The first stage's grader validation (planted errors, rewordings, injection, preference judging) and its 35-row failure triage are on their own page: First stage →. Nothing from it is pooled with this run.

Before part 1

The slice and how I chose it

30 tasks, 6 occupations, chosen by rule, pre-registered. The first stage's three occupations (all 15 of their gold tasks) plus three more picked by a mechanical rule before any new output existed: every other occupation whose five gold deliverables are all native Word or Excel (two qualify: Production Supervisors, Order Clerks), then one from the four-of-five tier, ties broken by sharing a sector with the first three (Accountants and Auditors). The four tasks the first stage had skipped now run: two PowerPoint deliverables and two that need live web research. Five predictions, the random review sample (22 outputs, seed 20261106, stratified by occupation) and every spend limit were written down first: plan, methods. Deviations are logged in the plan.

The first stage's rule, also fixed before any output existed: all five gold tasks in each of three occupations from the GDPval gold set (revision 11e7900): Buyers and Purchasing Agents, Project Management Specialists, Shipping/Receiving/Inventory Clerks. Why these three: native Word and Excel deliverables whose approval routes and dependency rules can be checked line by line against source records, with concrete consequences (a release without Finance sign-off is an audit finding). Six occupations is not a random sample of GDPval's 44, so the page reports counts and intervals, never a GDPval-wide rate.

Part 3 · Gaps and opportunities

Where the models fell short, which gaps data can close, what the dataset must contain

Where the models fell short

  • The review identified consequential errors in 7 of 22 sampled outputs (32%, 95% CI 16–53%): 6 of 10 in purchasing, shipping and project work, 1 of 12 in production supervision, order processing and accounting.
  • The errors sit where the document commits someone: a cost total understated by ₹12m, notices reported as sent, a price increase released with the program manager only informed, an extra signature that would hold approved changes, a receipt halved twice, a non-standard carrier, a purposive sample projected to the population (Part 2).
  • One deliverable reports an action that did not happen: Opus told the CPO that stop-work and refund notices had gone out; the files show only the decision (Part 2).
  • Delivery and formatting were not the problem: 132 of 132 deliverables produced; in the first stage 53 of 53 parseable outputs passed the targeted fact checks.
  • Sonnet 5 of 12, Opus 2 of 10: too few to rank the two models.
  • The first stage's signature (rule stated correctly, broken in the operative clause) appears in 2 of the 7; it is one route to a consequential error, not the main one.

What such a dataset would need to contain, in one list

  1. Task families written by working professionals, with a validated contract (prompt, files and rubric agree) and an expert-solved reference deliverable in native format.
  2. Per-requirement rubrics with a stated check and a followed check across every operative section.
  3. For every model output read: a located label (quoted line, contradicting source, what acting on it costs) and a severity on a scale calibrated against outcomes.
  4. Accept labels on outputs judges fail, so false-alarm rates are measured, plus masked preference pairs with rationales.
  5. Workspaces with approved and draft records, missing facts and controlled variants; positive controls where acting is correct.
  6. Fresh private families, split train / dev / sealed test by template, with a second reader on a fixed share of labels and an agreement statistic.

Future experiments, training hypotheses and planning assumptions

What the labels become for training

A hypothesis, since no training was run here, with the cheapest test for each line.

  • Located critiques → a critic or process-reward model. Train a judge on (output, failed requirement, quoted line, contradicting source, severity). Test: located-error recall on sealed families against the untrained judges here (1 of 7 in a scored requirement; 5 of 7 in the GPT-6.1 judge's verdict text), and false-alarm rate against the accept labels. Test size below.
  • Preference pairs with rationales → a reward model. Pairs where the preferred output is the one without the consequential error, with the rationale citing the located label. Test: does the reward model stop preferring the broken document (it did so 3 of 6 times here where the rubric disagreed)?
  • Workspaces with conflicting records → RL environments. The approval state and the record registry make the outcome checkable: did the released document keep every required approver, did it commit to stock that was never counted? Positive controls make refusal lose. Test: consequential-error rate and unnecessary-hold rate on sealed families, reported together.
  • Stated/followed pairs → a consistency objective. Outputs that state the rule and break it in the operative clause are negatives with a precise locator; the first stage found this in all 4 of its confirmed errors, this run in 2 of 7.

Honest about the limits

Sample size, grader reliability, cost

  • How the 27 new labels were made. An AI reviewer drafted the labels from packets containing the request and extracted source and output text. Judge comments were withheld. I checked each draft against its packet, spending about three hours in total, and confirmed all 27. They are one reviewer's check: no second reader, so no agreement statistic. The records carry one verification timestamp and no per-label minutes. I am not a practitioner in these occupations, so these are an AI draft with one non-expert check. The drafting model is from the same developer as the GPT-6.1 judge, which could tilt judge-versus-label comparisons toward that judge: the severity-4 result (0 of 7, both judges) does not depend on that; the 5-of-7 verdict count may.
  • Workbook excerpts in the review packets. In 9 of the 22 sampled outputs, one workbook exceeded the packet limit. For each of those workbooks, the packet included the first 80,000 characters of extracted text. The review identified a consequential error in one of the nine outputs. For the other eight, the finding applies to the material reviewed. The records do not establish whether the author separately inspected the rest of each workbook. The packet cut each output at 80,000 characters and each source at 60,000, with no marker; all 9 are spreadsheets, the largest 4,877,308 characters, and the 8 without a confirmed error are among the 15 with no consequential error identified during review, so the 12-of-15 count of judge flags on outputs with no consequential error identified rests partly on excerpts. The packet named the solver model: blind to judge text, not to the solver.
  • The sample freeze. Frozen before this run's grading, not before all grading: 7 of the 22 outputs came from the earlier follow-up run and already had judge files on disk, which the reviewer never opened; only aggregate counts had been published. 5 of the 7 confirmed errors are among those 7 outputs, all from the original three occupations.
  • Three readings drafted with AI assistance. Where a judge names the reviewer's error (read over the whole judgment), the category of each judge flag on outputs with no consequential error identified, and which errors misreport what was done or known were read from the texts with AI assistance; each table says whether I have confirmed it row by row. The counts under the three decision rules are computed from the judgment files and need no reading.
  • Three web-research outputs in the sample. The pre-registration reports web-research tasks separately, but the random sample was drawn from all 30 tasks, so three of the 22 are web-research outputs and one of the seven errors (the carrier workbook) is among them. Both figures are given: 7 of 22, and 6 of 19 (CI 15–54%) on native-file tasks only.
  • Small random sample. The review identified errors in 7 of 22 outputs. The 16–53% Wilson interval is a descriptive binomial calculation conditional on those labels. It omits error from incomplete inspection and reviewer disagreement and does not adjust for unequal inclusion probabilities in the stratified draw. It should not be interpreted as a calibrated interval for all 58 eligible outputs or GDPval. Per-occupation samples contain only 3 or 4 outputs.
  • Grader coverage. The GPT-6.1 judge graded only the 15 new outputs in the random sample (cost); Gemini graded all 38. Of the 53 new grades, 16 saw a partial view of a data-dump spreadsheet under the output budget (flagged per task), and none passes the strict evidence check, because the grading prompt's output format omits the location field the checker requires; verdicts are read from the judgment, as in the first stage, with that stated. Repeats were produced (66 of 66) but not graded. The full-rubric check covers 6 of 8 confirmed errors.
  • Underpowered predictions. P1 could only be tested on the 12 labeled new-occupation outputs, which hold one confirmed error; P3 held for the GPT-6.1 judge on a single pair. Both are reported as registered, as weak tests.
  • The first stage (own page) is small, one reviewer, purposive: 8 full-output reviews, 4 consequential errors confirmed, 18 candidates unreviewed; a different harness, never pooled with this run; 10 of 27–66 rubric items judged per output; strict quote validity 7/28 (Gemini) and 10/28 (GPT-6.1).
  • No training was run. The training section is a hypothesis with a test per line, not a result.
  • Revised after review. An earlier version of this page led with "the judges see the error, not the stakes" on the severity-4 count alone. An external read of the judgment files showed that the failed requirement was usually not the reviewer's error, and that the grader's pre-registered rule (critical requirement or severity ≥ 4) gives different counts. The headline, Part 2 and the decision-rule charts were rewritten on that comparison. A second external read then showed that the comparison had read only the failed requirements: the GPT-6.1 judge names 4 more of the 7 errors in its overall verdict, outside any requirement and without a severity. The located count, the headline and Part 2 were redone over the whole judgment (a drafted reading, see above). Nothing was regraded or relabeled.
